As we are approaching Thanksgiving in the US, it’s a time of reflection for many of us to consider what we are grateful for in our lives. Amongst the many blessings I have received in my personal life, I recently began to be particularly grateful for my career timing in software engineering. I entered my career a few years before AI as we know it today existed and have realized that students, interns, and junior engineers will never know what it was like to work in the industry without these tools.

I began my programming career in May of 2018 where I worked at a very small engineering firm that did industrial automation. I remember the first day of my internship; my boss sent me a PDF with over 1000 pages on this Fanuc robotic arm. He sent me the link to [ros.org](https://www.ros.org) where I was overwhelmed by the amount of information I had to learn just to get this big yellow arm to move. It was a jolting but exhilarating experience, as by the end of the first week, I was writing C++ code that sent instructions to this arm that made it move. I was so proud that I had managed to suss out what I needed to know from forums, documentation, FOSS repos, and asking my manager and one other engineer on the team.

This experience of having to solve *the hard problem* mostly continued in my roles after this. I felt very accomplished and proud to work at startups and smaller companies where I was a critical part of the engineering team even as someone with very little full-time professional experience. Fast forward to April 2022 and I was thrust into the most intense setting of my career so far. The term “drinking from a fire hose” is overused, but in this case, it really was an overwhelming amount of information to absorb in a small amount of time. However, I had a huge team that supported me to get the answers I needed. During my 3-year tenure at Apple, use of AI tools was strictly prohibited. This meant that I had to do everything the old-fashioned way while working on products and features that billions of people would use.

When you work at a company like Apple, everyone is on a need-to-know basis, and many of us were working on things that you could not discuss with anyone outside a handful of people. This made *the hard problem* even harder as you had to find the right solution from the right people and only give them the necessary information to make progress. At times this was very frustrating as it seemed like a waste of time, but the effect it had was it made me a much better communicator on my team and in the company. Before I asked a question, I had to understand the root of the problem of exactly where I was stuck, why the current tools didn’t work, and find out who knew the most about this problem at the company.

Many mid-level and senior engineers mention how much less friction there is now that LLMs allow them to look up solutions faster, generate a tremendous amount of code with one prompt, and use agents to implement entire features. I still haven’t been overly impressed with these agents, and I worry about our ability as engineers to communicate with other humans and the erosion of our skills. Whatever the future holds, I am incredibly grateful for the skills I have acquired during the last 7 years.
